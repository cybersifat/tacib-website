PRACTICAL CYBER SAFETY
GUIDE · EDUCATION

Before running a security test.

A tool being available does not mean a target is in scope. Responsible security testing begins with permission, boundaries, communication, and a plan to limit harm.

1. Confirm authorization in writing

Get confirmation from the system owner or a person with documented authority to approve the test. A public IP address, bug-report email address, or general claim of “ethical hacking” is not permission.

2. Write a clear scope

  • List exact domains, IP addresses, applications, accounts, or environments authorized for testing.
  • Define permitted and prohibited techniques, times, source addresses, and rate limits.
  • Specify whether authentication testing, social engineering, denial-of-service tests, or exploitation are excluded. Treat these as prohibited unless explicitly approved.
  • Set a stop condition and an emergency contact if anything unexpected happens.

3. Minimize data and disruption

Use the least invasive validation that reasonably establishes the issue. Do not download user data, access unrelated accounts, maintain persistence, or alter production data to make a point. Stop if testing creates unexpected impact.

4. Report findings carefully

Document the affected asset, impact, conditions, minimal reproduction, and remediation guidance. Remove unnecessary sensitive data from screenshots and logs. Share the report only with authorized recipients.

5. Disclose responsibly

Follow the system owner's disclosure policy. Allow appropriate time for validation and remediation; coordinate public disclosure to reduce risk. Do not threaten disclosure in exchange for money, access, or special treatment.

Never treat this guide as authorization. If you do not own the system or have explicit permission, use a legal training lab or an authorized bug-bounty scope instead.
